*Most vendors publish customer success stories, but buyers are looking for case studies, and there is a difference. Here's how to give buyers what they need, based on insights from our team who have been on both sides of the table.*

Buyers seek out case studies to build trust and reduce risk, while analysts, advisors, and influencers use them to validate the category, and when surveyed, marketers say they are essential for building credibility.[^1]

Case studies are not only a fantastic asset in the closing stages of a buyer's journey, but what you discover in a well-run case study process could be gold for boosting awareness of your solution amongst analysts and industry influencers, as you will uncover the statistics and quotes that support their market intelligence and point of view.

No surprises there; it’s been true for decades, but two things have changed.

* **Buyers now do most of the work before they talk to you:** buying groups are deep into the selection process, with a shortlist and often a favorite, before they contact a vendor, and that favorite wins 82% of the time.[^2]
* **AI assistants have joined the buying group**, researching, summarizing, and comparing before a shortlist is drawn up.[^3]

A good case study does its work while you're not in the room, and a machine often reads it first.

Yet many fall short. They're customer success stories rather than case studies, a simple story that goes something like: there was a problem, the vendor fixed it, and there was a happy ending. They offer little helpful advice or insight the buyer can use to make the decision or trust you with it.

That's a lost opportunity, given how hard-won permission to do a case study usually is.

## What makes a good case study?

A good case study builds trust and credibility by expressing the vendor's value and proposition in an authentic buyer voice, with tangible outcomes specific to their industry or use case. They are also incredibly useful for buyers looking to match their problem with a solution, reassuring them they are moving in the right direction.

Whilst we advise making the client the hero, we have one word of warning.  It's easy to lose your content marketing discipline and get distracted by the joy of a client agreeing to say lovely things about your product, indulging them with a long, meandering story of **their** 900-year history, showing beautiful panned shots of their HQ, or highlighting **their** differentiation in **their** marketplace. Your intended audience will sadly care less about this, and it won't help you.

A good case study sticks to the problem at hand, tells the buyer how the decision was made, what was hard, and what changed, in numbers they can trust and in the customer's own voice.

## #1 – Pack for the long road

The process of getting the case study is likely to be a long one, gated with several approvals, and it's often hard to go back through these gates once something is approved. So plan for the journey.

Therefore, it's essential that from the start you have a clear plan of what you need from the client — what does the case study look like in your mind, how do you intend to use it, what would make it useful to your audience?

Once approval comes, the temptation to "just get something going" will be strong, but this is the opportunity to get agreement on the whole plan and set the expectation with the client of what you would like to achieve, and you'll get a much better content asset.

For example, videos are the gold standard as they combine high-performing content (a case study) with a high-performing medium and distribution channel (video). I've done a bunch of them, from very short YouTube interviews to fully produced stories. Get this in the plan and approved early, however daunting the approval process and logistics may seem.

## #2 – Tell a useful story, not a fairy story

A case study is a partnership story with two protagonists: the hero (the client) and their sidekick (your product or service).

Think through the story; you probably know the end, the hero disappearing into the sunset with a successful project behind them, offering to give you a case study.

What were the start and the middle? What was the catalyst for change, what happened along the way, and what could other clients learn from that journey?

Often we start the story with the problem identification and the decision, but there is a bit in between that buyers need: how did the hero of our story get the project started? Who did they need in the room, what alternatives did they consider, what tipped the scales, and how did the champion sell it internally?

You’ve seen this from the vendor side; you know that in most buying processes, there are challenging moments. Gartner research found that 74% of B2B buying teams had unhealthy conflict[^4] once the process got underway.

The primary purpose of the case study is to mitigate buyer risk by addressing the fear of choosing the wrong solution. However, the buyer's fear is deeper than that. The buyer's real fear is often the change required, the implementation, and what it takes to solve their problem; most buyers know trouble lies ahead.

A different Gartner study found that 79% of technology buyers experience regret related to challenges that span from bad requirements to insufficient contracts and bad implementations.[^5]

This is the part of the story the reader can reuse for their own decision, and yet a vendor case study rarely includes any of this; doing so would differentiate your content from the rest.

## #3 – Data is the daddy

The story ends with an outcome, and this needs to be clear, not just the sunset and "we were all jolly happy." By what metric did your solution help this client?

The most important thing to capture in a case study is the numbers. Numbers are easy to consume and are nectar to our audience of busy, busy bees, and they’re what will interest your next client or industry influencer.

Numbers are also easy to repurpose, not just by you in your next infographic, but by your audience, as they make the internal case for your solution. Plus, industry influencers are always on the hunt for data that supports their point of view on the market or category to include in a blog post, research article, or next book.

However, these numbers need to be validated, trustworthy, and specific, or they will just sound like spin. If the claim is that the process was 40% more efficient, what was the baseline and what did this mean in concrete terms?

If you can't get numbers, it's lovely that this client wants to talk about you in general terms, but if you have other clients in the program, consider moving on, as nicely as you can, to one with a firmer story. Case studies take a lot of effort, and you should invest it wisely.

## #4 – Prepare for the slice and dice

Ensure that every element of the case study is authorized to be used separately from the combined deliverable. Especially the numbers, per the previous point, but also quotes — deliciously snackable, reusable content that will live long after you've told the case study story, and your sales folks will love slipping them into conversation and their slides.

Get the use of quotes approved early, get a few versions approved based on the context you'd like to use them in, and ensure they are approved independently of the overall case study so you can use them in other marketing materials.

But not just quotes — ensure all the images, copy, logos, facts, figures, and summary text, whatever slices and dices you can think of, are approved to be used independently of the whole. Trust me, your future self will thank you. Going back to get approval is frankly a nightmare. People change jobs, new people join legal, policies get altered, or maybe at the moment you need the change, the project just isn't going as well as it was.

## #5 – Managing no

Sometimes they say no, and this isn't one of those "ask forgiveness later" deals. A poorly used reference or logo, with the wrong audience, can embarrass your client stakeholder, ruin a client relationship, and kill credibility with a prospective client or analyst.

However, approval can come in subtly different forms.

For example, some clients will agree you can talk about them in a closed meeting but won't let you put their logo on your website, or they’ll attend a roundtable but won’t speak on stage or give a testimonial. While you might be disappointed as a marketer, this could be the start of the dance and as much about their personal fears as about corporate policy.

So, start the process with a sliding scale of what you'd like from the client. From a video interview, through use of the logo on the website, to an anecdote for a closed room, or whatever else you need.

Then ensure that what can be shared, and with whom, is clearly understood by everyone who comes into contact with the case study material.

## Bonus Tip: Peer to peer

Nearly three-quarters of decision-makers trust peer insights above vendor websites,[^6] and as a vendor, you have limited options to influence third-party reviews, but a strong case study can help build peer trust.

Vendors can also learn from this for owned case studies. Case studies must be written as a peer, plainly and as objectively as possible, giving the customer’s voice a platform, not the vendor’s and especially not sales, using the language of the buyer and the practitioner, not the buzzwords of the category.

Better yet, have it written by a third party, who will also have the advantage of disarming a cautious client, who might share more, and of diving into the messy bits that the buyers need, but are uncomfortable for vendors to say out loud, and are sometimes tricky to get internally approved.

Those are a few insider tips. A strong case study takes time and needs planning and persistence. But get on the long road, create a credible story (not a fairy story) with great numbers, shareable quotes, proper approval, and snippets you can sprinkle into other marketing and sales activities, and you'll be rewarded with a useful, high-performing content asset.
