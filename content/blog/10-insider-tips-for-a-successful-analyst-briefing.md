*Despite the rise of AI, and before that, peer reviews, social selling and content marketing, the big analyst firms like Forrester, Gartner and IDC are still hugely influential in shaping software categories and markets, especially in the risk-averse world of the big corporates. Here are some tips from someone who’s seen their fair share of briefings, both good and bad.*

---

Cynics might suggest analyst relations is simple – it's “pay to play”, but smaller vendors can brief analysts. Access and influence aren't just for those paying a hefty subscription; in fact, those who treat their seat in this way are missing the point (but that’s for another post). It's an analyst's job to be curious and understand the market, and they will usually welcome the conversation.

Of course, this is only a briefing; you won't be able to ask questions or get any feedback, although an enthusiastic analyst may not be able to help themselves — clarifying questions can go a long way. If you want to get properly engaged, you **will** need to subscribe.

But first, there is the briefing, and here are ten tips I've learned from a couple of decades of analyst relations experience, from both sides of the table:

## #1 – Don't sell the pain

It's a sales pitch, and it isn't. Good sales practice is that you need to market and sell the problem you are solving to discover pain in an organization. So a pitch to a client includes a chunk of the presentation talking about the change in the world, client education, or discovery of the pain. A good analyst already knows the problem the solutions in their category solve, and will hear this in every briefing — so skip it and focus on you.

## #2 – Focus on outcomes

OK, maybe not focus on *you* — all that stuff about the year the company started, the number of offices or staff you have, or even your approach to the problem, comes later. Focus on tangible outcomes your clients have seen through great case studies (and we have [some tips on creating case studies](https://www.martechinsiders.com/blog/5-insider-tips-for-a-great-case-study)). Analysts crave case studies as they validate not only you but also their research and the benefits of the category as a whole. This then gives you permission to tell the story of how that sausage got its sizzle, how you achieved that outcome, and talk about you.

## #3 – Focus on the difference

As with client pitches, while the products we market and sell have got way more complicated and feature-rich, the time we have to demonstrate them remains the same. Don't focus on whatever the "table stakes" are for your industry category; focus on what's different about your product, point of view, or approach.

## #4 – Be authentic

Analysts are wary, even skeptical, of bold claims, and it's easy to get busted. If you share figures, make sure they have proper attribution. Be clear about which part of that huge organization you claim to be working with is using your solution (if it's a big brand, they may know someone). And analysts know that no solution is perfect, so be human about it — turn the selling down a little.

## #5 – The analyst knows the competitors better than you do

Tricky one, this. If the analyst doesn't know you, it's natural to frame your company within the category using competitors — "we are like X, only different." It's easy to think you know the competition well, but be careful what you say. You could end up undermining your credibility if you get this wrong, seem arrogant about a vendor the analyst rates highly, or distract the analyst into debating the competition instead of focusing on you. Let your case studies speak to the competition and who you were replacing to build credibility.

## #6 – There are always competitors

Following on from the last point, it's tempting to say there are no competitors because you feel strongly your approach is unique. That might be true, but if you're briefing a significant analyst firm, the category will be mature enough to have other players — even if, in your view, they're using the wrong product as a workaround. Your job is to brief the analyst to consider your points of difference, their importance in defining the category, and in how they judge those other solutions.

## #7 – Respect the analyst and their work

Look the analyst up on LinkedIn (the whole briefing team should), read the research you can get access to, and think about your point of view on that research so you can demonstrate you've read it. Once you've done your research, you can frame your briefing around the things they care about and be immediately relevant. Firms will all have differing definitions and terminology for the same functionality so be sure to adapt and speak their language. And don't quote other analysts or rival firms — not just because it's bad form to mention a competitor, but many analysts are sensitive about following other analysts.

## #8 – Don't boil the ocean

An analyst briefing is just part of an influencer journey. If it's your first, it's an introduction that should leave them wanting more — don't try to cram everything into 100 slides and race through it. Consider the few points you'd like the analyst to take away from each session.

## #9 – Think about what the analyst wants

Analysts are humans, trying to hustle along just like you. They have targets, goals, research to complete, and probably competition for credibility in their coverage area. Think about how you can help with that — can you introduce them to a client that can help their research, share a case study that validates the category, or point them to some independent research?

## #10 – Demo the product (and make sure it works)

Weird to have to write this, but as an analyst I saw many vendors show up with slides and spend the briefing talking about the problem and their approach — we had to tell vendors we wanted to see the product when we booked the briefings. When conducting a demo, keep it tight and relevant to the analyst's area of research, the points you want to get across, and your differentiation.

Avoid the harbor tour of clicking on every feature, or insisting on showing the parts of the demo you're most proud of or spent all night preparing if they aren't relevant. Trust me, I've been there.

**Bonus tip:** This is the first date in what may become a long relationship, both for you personally and for your company. Don't just show up for scheduled briefings and do nothing in between — nurture the relationship on social and with email updates when you have something worth sharing.
